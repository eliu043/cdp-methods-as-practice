# Lots in Commons

## Seven-minute presentation script and editorial notes

**Status:** Working narrative, second pass

**Deck:** 9 vertically navigated slides

**Target:** 6:45–7:00 at approximately 135 words per minute

**Long-form archive:** `presentation-narrative-and-editorial-notes-long-form.md`

## The argument in one sentence

Forecasts do not merely describe uncertain futures: institutions use them to reorganize land, capacity, value, and perception in the present; this project asks whether situated sensing and representational friction can keep those translations open to contest.

## Three threads—and the question that binds them

1. **Prediction becomes infrastructure.** Predictive markets organize expectations; Keller Easterling helps ask how those expectations acquire spatial agency through standards, incentives, zones, and repeatable formulas.
2. **Other ways of knowing remain active.** Donna Haraway, Anna Tsing, and Nora N. Khan help resist the idea that partiality is simply a defect to be engineered away.
3. **Images do work.** Hito Steyerl and Jussi Parikka help shift the image from representation alone toward circulation, classification, measurement, and action.

The shared question is not whether computation is good or bad. It is: **which ways of knowing gain the authority to organize matter, which are treated as remainder, and how might representation redistribute that authority?**

## Slide 1 — Lots in Commons

**Time:** 0:00–0:35

**Visual role:** Establish the title as an open territory. No explanatory precedent is needed.

### Script

*Lots in Commons* is a working title for a research territory. “Lots” names parcels and abundance, but also division. “Commons” names the systems collective life relies upon—even when their governance is anything but common.

The project begins with an unease: uncertain futures become concrete long before they become certain. An expectation becomes a line on a map; soon the line becomes a contract, a substation, a landscape. I want to follow that hardening and ask how it might remain negotiable. The scale shifts from planetary systems to institutional protocols. The question stays close to the ground.

### Editorial note

Keep the cover visually quiet. The future poster can begin to enter as a faint vertical trace, but it should not yet resolve into a diagram.

## Slide 2 — Forecasts are infrastructure before they are buildings

**Time:** 0:35–1:25

**Visual role:** Paglen grounds an apparently planetary network; the ERCOT map shows capacity as an actionable field.

### Script

Infrastructure is usually treated as background. I approach it as the medium where matter, law, finance, and expectation meet. A forecast does not simply report a possible future. It changes what appears investable, what receives permission, and what is prepared to connect.

Keller Easterling’s *Extrastatecraft* gives me a language for this spatial agency. Markets price expected demand, but infrastructure space carries that expectation outward. It settles into standards and repeatable formulas until a speculative future begins to look like an ordinary course of action.

Paglen’s cable landing sites and the ERCOT map show the moment this abstraction touches ground. Prediction becomes infrastructural when institutions can act through it before the event it anticipates has arrived.

### Editorial note

Do not claim that Easterling writes directly about prediction markets. Her role is to explain the infrastructure space through which priced expectations become permissions and material commitments. One network image and one grounded site are enough. Distinguish a forecast from a scenario, target, request, or promise only if asked.

## Slide 3 — Prediction becomes material through translation

**Time:** 1:25–2:20

**Visual role:** The network is the project’s analytical diagram. Each translation separates category, process, and actor/instrument into linked nodes; the four branches converge on one shared site while the outer connections make circular causality visible.

### Script

The primary question is: how do institutions translate uncertain futures into sites and material commitments?

I am following four interacting translations. A projection becomes an obligation. Ground becomes value. Terrain becomes a score. A resource becomes a promise.

These are not neutral stages; they reinforce one another. Demand justifies capacity. Promised capacity alters land value. Value directs selection, and the selected site produces a new story of demand that appears to confirm where the cycle began.

At every turn, authority is redistributed. Who produces the forecast? Who can act on it, who absorbs its consequences, and who may object? Beneath the network lies a remainder: everything that persists after it stops counting as evidence.

### Editorial note

Let the network carry the causal structure. Pause after the main question, then follow one branch from category to process to actor/instrument before moving quickly through the other three. Read the bottom actor question aloud rather than narrating every node.

## Slide 4 — North Texas is an aperture, not a model

**Time:** 2:20–3:00

**Visual role:** Move from institutional vision to embodied and material sensing.

### Script

North Texas remains useful as an aperture rather than a universal case. At its suburban–rural edge, competing futures for the same ground become unusually visible. A field can be read as agriculture, subdivision, logistics territory, or computational capacity.

The question becomes one of sensing. Administrative maps register parcels with precision while flattening what moves beneath and across them. Anthony Acciavatti’s *Grounded Growth* treats groundwater as urban form rather than hidden resource. The wearable apparatus brings the sensing body into the frame. Together they ask what becomes perceptible when measurement is situated—and whose exposure counts as evidence.

### Editorial note

Confirm the author and title of the wearable apparatus before a formal review. Until then, keep its caption descriptive rather than authoritative.

## Slide 5 — A forecast is only one way of knowing a future

**Time:** 3:00–3:55

**Visual role:** A distributed collage connects ecological counter-reading, limits of computation, and material transformation without treating them as equal categories.

### Script

The contrast is not rational computation versus irrational ecology. It is a difference in attention. A forecast isolates variables, prices risk, and searches for what can be repeated. Haraway asks us to remain with damaged entanglements. Tsing follows relations that resist scalability and nevertheless sustain life.

Nora Khan’s *HOLO 3: Mirror Stage* brings that tension inside computation. It attends to the distance between a machine-learning hypothetical and a lived world that remains partial and difficult to explain. Modern alchemy is not an escape into mysticism. It is a willingness to work obliquely with transformation—and resist the demand that uncertainty must always be resolved.

*Rare Earthenware* makes the problem tangible. Mine waste becomes a vessel, but its toxicity remains. Transformation carries its remainder.

### Editorial note

Avoid a catalogue of theorists in speech. Haraway and Tsing establish the ecological counter-reading; Khan’s four-part editorial structure—partial knowing, prediction, language, and explainability—provides the bridge back to computation. Keep Glissant, Stengers, Hui, and Campagna in the Field for later development.

## Slide 6 — When models train on models, human data becomes scarce ground truth

**Time:** 3:55–4:45

**Visual role:** The *Anatomy of AI* diagram shows material systems; *ImageNet Roulette* shows recognition as classification and harm.

### Script

The question of value returns in AI training. Human expression becomes raw material; generated output circulates back into the corpus. Institutions then decide which human traces still qualify as provenance or ground truth.

Research on model collapse shows that indiscriminate recursion can narrow a distribution until its edges disappear. Collapse is not inevitable when original data is retained and mixtures are curated—but that makes the politics sharper. Who controls the mixture? Who defines the real?

*ImageNet Roulette* reminds us that legibility is not automatically liberating. Recognition can misname and injure. Agency may mean correction; sometimes it may mean the right to remain unread.

### Editorial note

Use “can,” not “will,” when discussing collapse. This keeps the claim technically defensible and focuses the slide on curation and authority.

## Slide 7 — What an image fails to resolve may still be evidence

**Time:** 4:45–5:40

**Visual role:** A three-part collage links glitch, atmospheric evidence, and machine-operational vision.

### Script

Representation is not downstream from these systems. Images participate in decisions about the world. Jussi Parikka extends the operational image toward pictures made by and for systems to act, often without a human viewer. Sensing stretches beyond the visible, making certain relations computable while others fall away.

Hito Steyerl’s poor image introduces a different politics. Low resolution is not only loss; it records the pressure of circulation and value. Omar Mismar lets corruption break across the image’s surface. *Cloud Studies* works in the other direction, gathering atmospheric traces until violence becomes perceptible.

Glitch should not be romanticized, but it can reveal the seam where signal becomes decision. Can remote sensing be reclaimed as situated witnessing rather than extraction?

### Editorial note

This is the poetic hinge of the presentation. Let the images remain visible after the final question; do not narrate every tile.

## Slide 8 — Composition becomes a method of investigation

**Time:** 5:40–6:30

**Visual role:** Present image, drawing, model, sculpture, and material experiment as distinct but interacting research instruments.

### Script

The project may take form less as an atlas than as a composition. A drawing can trace what an image cannot hold. A model gives that relation weight and duration. An interface rearranges the terms—and exposes when one account begins to dominate another.

*Computational Compost* does not simply represent infrastructure. It makes computation encounter metabolism and living matter within one experiment. The argument emerges from their contact.

I want to work similarly: not illustrating a thesis already known, but letting the research change as it moves across screen, paper, and matter. The composition becomes the place where evidence is staged, propositions tested, and new questions allowed to appear.

### Editorial note

Do not turn this slide into an inventory of media. *Computational Compost* matters because its media form one experiment. Replace the generic media labels with fragments of the project’s own drawings, models, and material studies as those are produced.

## Slide 9 — Open proposition

**Time:** 6:30–6:55

**Visual role:** End on one question and white space.

### Script

The project does not propose that every forecast should stop, or that every system can remain reversible. It asks what happens at the moment uncertainty hardens into authority—and whether representation can reopen that moment.

The working proposition is therefore: **how might representation keep a future contestable before prediction becomes commitment?**

### Editorial note

Stop after the question. The Field and Index links can remain visible for discussion but should not be verbally introduced.
