# Lots in Commons

## Presentation narrative and editorial notes

**Status:** Working document for research-territory presentation  
**Prepared:** 2 August 2026  
**Current deck:** 13 slides, navigated vertically  
**Suggested use:** Speaker notes, editorial checklist, and a record of unresolved research decisions

This document intentionally does not force the project into a settled thesis. The presentation is strongest when it establishes a consequential research territory: infrastructures anticipate futures; forecasts obtain institutional authority; landscapes are translated into actionable sites; and design might expose or interrupt those translations. North Texas can remain a productive aperture into that territory without becoming its permanent boundary.

The scripts below are written for speech rather than direct placement on the slides. At a measured pace, the complete sequence is approximately 14–17 minutes. For a shorter presentation, use the first paragraph of each script and treat the second paragraph as optional elaboration.

---

## Editorial position across the deck

### What is already working

The deck has a clear causal spine:

> infrastructure → forecast → translation → site → commitment → possible interruption

This is more persuasive than organizing the project as a list of themes. It allows the audience to understand not only what the research is about, but where power operates within the process.

The title **Lots in Commons** is usefully multivalent. “Lots” names parcels, abundance, and multiplicity; “commons” names both a contested political horizon and the shared systems through which contemporary life is sustained. The subtitle, **Speculations on Shared Infrastructural Life**, appropriately keeps the research open.

### The principal tension to retain—but stage more deliberately

The deck moves between two scales of inquiry:

1. a broad investigation of planetary infrastructure, computation, prediction, and counter-knowledge; and
2. a more specific inquiry into North Texas, suburban development, data centers, and land conversion.

This is not inherently a problem. A research territory needs an aperture. The issue is rhetorical: the audience should know when North Texas is being offered as a **provisional test site**, rather than encountering it as an unexplained return to a thesis the project has already left behind.

### Three principles for the next revision

1. **Every image should perform an operation.** It should reveal a scale, expose a protocol, introduce evidence, or demonstrate a representational method. A website screenshot rarely accomplishes this by itself.
2. **Every precedent should yield a transferable method.** Name what the project borrows: a form of tracing, an evidentiary convention, a mode of navigation, a refusal, or a relationship between media.
3. **Quotes should complicate the project’s claim.** They should not serve as intellectual decoration or as external confirmation of something the project can state in its own voice.

# Slide-by-slide narrative and critique

## Slide 1 — Opening

### Current heading

**Lots in Commons**  
*Speculations on Shared Infrastructural Life*

### Function in the argument

Establish the project as a field of inquiry rather than a completed thesis, and define “infrastructure” as a condition of collective life rather than only a technical object.

### Speaker script

This project begins with an apparent contradiction. Infrastructure is shared in the sense that it organizes collective life, yet the systems on which that life depends are rarely held, perceived, or governed in common. Land, water, energy, computation, logistics, and finance are connected through arrangements that exceed any single site, institution, or public. They distribute capacities and risks unevenly while often appearing merely technical.

**Lots in Commons** is therefore not yet the name of a singular object. It is a research territory concerned with how shared worlds are divided into lots, loads, service areas, risk classes, and development opportunities—and with how those divisions might be represented otherwise. The project moves among planetary systems, situated evidence, institutional protocols, and speculative forms of negotiation. Its purpose at this stage is to identify where a thesis might take hold without pretending that the territory has already been resolved.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- The large title and generous field of white space. The opening should not attempt to summarize all subsequent slides.
- The subtitle. It is broad enough to accommodate different thesis trajectories.
- The phrase “research structure” in the small note; it accurately describes the project’s present status.

**Change**

- The cover note currently lists four scales of concern but does not name the conflict connecting them. Consider: “A research structure for following how uncertain futures become material commitments—and how those commitments might remain contestable.”
- In speech, define “commons” cautiously. Do not imply that infrastructure is already a commons; identify commoning as an unresolved political and design question.

**Add**

- If an image is introduced, use only one faint, partial fragment: a parcel boundary crossing a watershed or utility territory, for example. It should suggest incompatible systems without becoming a hero image.
- A very small version/date marker could help distinguish this open research-territory deck from a future thesis defense.

**Remove or avoid**

- Avoid planetary satellite imagery, server racks, or generic aerial suburban photography on the cover. These would prematurely announce a subject and flatten the project into a familiar visual genre.

## Slide 2 — Premise

### Current heading

**Infrastructure is not a background.**

### Function in the argument

Reframe infrastructure as an active political and material arrangement rather than a neutral support system.

### Speaker script

Infrastructure is often treated as background: a substrate that becomes noticeable only when it fails. I am approaching it instead as an active arrangement of matter, labor, measurement, law, finance, and expectation. A grid is not simply a technical network; it is also a geography of investment and exclusion. A parcel is not simply an area of ground; it is an achievement of survey, title, valuation, and enforcement. A data center is not simply a building; it is the local materialization of distant computational demand.

The research begins at moments when landscapes are made legible as capacity—when water becomes supply, land becomes inventory, electricity becomes available load, and a collective future becomes a forecast. These conversions do not simply describe what is already present. They reorganize what can be claimed, financed, permitted, and built. The first proposition, then, is that infrastructure does not sit behind social life. It actively formats the conditions under which particular forms of life become possible.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- The declarative heading. It is concise and gives the deck a strong opening proposition.
- *Anatomy of an AI System* as a precedent. Its supply-chain diagram supports the move from apparently immaterial computation to material, labor, and logistical systems.

**Change**

- Replace the remote webpage thumbnails with selected images from the actual projects, accompanied by concise source information. The images should let the audience see the methodological relation being claimed.
- The current slide says “matter, measurement, labor, law, and expectation,” while the body adds finance. Standardize this list or, better, use the differences to establish a set of recurring research lenses.

**Add**

- Add one institutional artifact beside the artistic precedent: a utility service-area map, a development agreement, a parcel plat, or a capacity chart. This would immediately establish that the project reads administrative documents as designed objects.
- The critical work of Deborah Cowen can strengthen the claim that infrastructure binds collective life while also producing disconnection, containment, and dispossession.
- Shannon Mattern’s *Code and Clay, Data and Dirt* is useful if the project wants to resist presenting mediated or “smart” territory as historically unprecedented.

**Remove or relocate**

- Trevor Paglen’s *Landing Sites* is relevant to hidden network geographies but does not yet clarify this premise. Move it to a slide about siting or methods unless a specific image can demonstrate how a network touches ground.

## Slide 3 — Projection

### Current heading

**Forecasts begin building before buildings do.**

### Function in the argument

Identify the project’s central mechanism: forecasts are performative devices that organize present commitments.

### Speaker script

Forecasts are commonly presented as neutral descriptions of a future that is expected to arrive. Yet population projections, electrical-load requests, traffic models, suitability scores, tax incentives, and infrastructure plans do more than anticipate growth. They help assemble the conditions under which growth becomes likely. Once projected demand enters a capital plan, land option, zoning amendment, or utility commitment, uncertainty begins to acquire physical form.

This does not mean that forecasts are simply false or manipulative. Their power lies precisely in the relationship between limited knowledge and consequential action. Institutions must act before the future is known, but those actions are not evenly reversible. A forecast may justify a road, substation, debt instrument, or land acquisition whose effects persist even if the anticipated demand changes. The project therefore asks not whether prediction is accurate in the abstract, but how a prediction becomes authorized, who can act through it, and which landscapes are required to absorb its uncertainty.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- This is the strongest heading in the deck and may eventually become a project title in its own right.
- Rotterdam Risk Scores and *The Library of Missing Datasets* are productive because they foreground classification and absence rather than merely illustrating infrastructure.
- The final question—“How does a forecast acquire material force?”—is an effective transition to the research questions.

**Change**

- Show one forecast beside one resulting commitment. For example: a load-growth chart beside a substation application; a population projection beside a capital-improvement boundary; a suitability surface beside a purchased parcel. The current precedents illustrate critique but not yet the mechanism.
- Distinguish a **forecast** from a **scenario**, **target**, **request**, and **promise**. These devices have different authors, evidentiary standards, and institutional effects.
- Consider using the phrase “performative forecast,” then explain it once in plain language rather than assuming the theoretical term is self-evident.

**Add**

- Louise Amoore’s *The Politics of Possibility* offers a useful account of political action organized around uncertain but consequential futures.
- Theodore Porter’s *Trust in Numbers* would help ground the authority of quantification institutionally rather than treating numbers as persuasive by themselves.
- Create a small causal sequence: `projection → authorization → commitment → material persistence`. It should remain visibly reversible or contested rather than appearing as an inevitable pipeline.

**Remove or relocate**

- Move the Glissant quotation to the counter-reading slide. Opacity is important, but here it interrupts the more precise question of how forecasts act. A slide should not borrow Glissant merely to suggest that models are incomplete.
- *Extrastatecraft* may work better on the premise or translations slide, where “infrastructure space” can be connected to repeatable institutional formulas.

## Slide 4 — Research questions

### Current heading

**What happens between prediction and construction?**

### Function in the argument

State a primary research question and show how subsidiary questions open several possible thesis paths.

### Speaker script

The research concentrates on the interval between prediction and construction. That interval is neither empty nor merely procedural. It contains the surveys, classifications, agreements, hearings, models, reservations, and financial decisions through which an uncertain future becomes a governable project. It is also where alternatives are progressively excluded, often before the public encounters a completed design.

The principal question is: **How do institutions translate uncertain futures into sites and commitments?** From that question follow several lines of inquiry. What architectures materialize demand that has not yet arrived? How do models of risk, suitability, and capacity assign value and distribute harm? What kinds of knowledge do those models omit by design? And can an interface make those omissions consequential—by introducing friction, obligation, doubt, or refusal—rather than merely representing them after a decision has been made?

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- One short, memorable question as the heading.
- The subsidiary questions in the right margin; visually, they appropriately read as tendrils rather than coequal theses.

**Change**

- Reduce the four numbered questions to one primary question and three operational questions. At present all four appear equally central, which makes the project seem broader than its method can sustain.
- Suggested primary question: **How do institutions translate uncertain futures into sites and material commitments?**
- Suggested operational questions:
  1. Which documents and protocols authorize the translation?
  2. What knowledge, duration, and obligation are excluded?
  3. How might a representational interface keep the translation contestable?

**Add**

- Add an actor question: who produces the forecast, who is empowered to act on it, who is asked to bear its consequences, and who has standing to contest it?
- Add a temporal question: which commitments can be revised, and which become effectively irreversible?

**Remove or defer**

- “What architectures materialize futures that have not yet arrived?” is evocative but presently functions more as a proposition than a research question. Retain it in the script or move it to the provisional-claim slide.
- “When should calculation stop?” is compelling but premature unless the project can name who stops it, under what authority, and with what consequence. Keep it as a research tendril, not yet as a promise.

## Slide 5 — Four translations

### Current heading

**A landscape becomes actionable through four translations.**

### Function in the argument

Offer the project’s clearest analytical framework: property, suitability, capacity, and demand.

### Speaker script

I am provisionally organizing the process of making a site through four translations. **Property** converts continuous and historically occupied ground into bounded title and exchangeable value. **Suitability** converts heterogeneous terrain into weighted evidence and a comparative score. **Capacity** converts water, electricity, roads, and fiber into services that can be allocated or promised. **Demand** converts an uncertain projection into an obligation to plan and build.

These translations are not merely changes in vocabulary. Each is performed through particular instruments, professions, laws, and visual conventions. They also overlap: a capacity promise can increase property value; a suitability score can erase an existing use; a demand forecast can trigger infrastructure that then appears to validate the forecast. The analytical task is therefore to reconstruct how these translations reinforce one another while remaining institutionally distinct. The project’s critical object may ultimately be less the completed building than this chain of conversions that makes a building appear reasonable, necessary, and locatable.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Retain the four translations. This is the most original and teachable framework in the current presentation.
- Keep the compact formulations: boundary → title → value; terrain → score → selection; resource → service → commitment; projection → obligation → build.

**Change**

- Avoid presenting the translations as a universal or strictly linear sequence. Show loops, conflicts, and feedback: promised capacity changes value; value influences selection; selected sites generate new demand narratives.
- Identify the agent and instrument beneath each translation: surveyor/plat; consultant/suitability model; utility/interconnection or service agreement; planning agency or developer/forecast.
- Test whether “demand” is actually the first translation rather than the last. The point is not to find a perfect order, but to make circular causality visible.

**Add**

- Use one concrete object across all four panels—ideally one parcel, corridor, or infrastructure proposal. A repeated object will prevent the framework from remaining abstract.
- Brenna Bhandar’s *Colonial Lives of Property* should complicate the apparently neutral movement from boundary to title by locating property abstraction within racial and colonial regimes.
- Add a thin fifth register beneath the four translations: **remainder**—what continues to exist but no longer counts as relevant evidence.

**Remove or avoid**

- Do not add more equal categories merely to be comprehensive. Labor, ecology, maintenance, and finance should cut across the four translations rather than become four more boxes.

## Slide 6 — Situated aperture

### Current heading

**North Texas makes these translations visible.**

### Function in the argument

Give the project enough ground to test its planetary claims while keeping the site provisional.

### Speaker script

Planetary infrastructure can become a view from nowhere. North Texas is useful because it gives these translations a provisional ground. Across the Dallas–Fort Worth development frontier, utility territories, watersheds, municipal boundaries, agricultural valuations, logistics corridors, subdivisions, and data-center campuses overlap without sharing a common frame of accountability.

Red Oak and Ellis County remain possible apertures rather than fixed conclusions. Their value lies in the adjacency of residential growth, agricultural land conversion, economic-development policy, and hyperscale computation. A situated inquiry could follow how one parcel or transect is described differently by a landowner, utility, municipal planner, developer, watershed, resident, and nonhuman ecology. The purpose of the site is not to claim that North Texas stands for the planet. It is to ask how planetary processes obtain specificity, authority, and material consequence somewhere.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep one situated slide. Without it, the project risks reproducing the totalizing planetary view it wants to critique.
- Retain the language of an “aperture.” It allows the site to focus the research without becoming its permanent limit.
- Keep the scale sequence from atmosphere to interface, but make it spatial rather than a collection of floating labels.

**Change**

- Decide which of two claims the slide makes:
  1. North Texas is the current empirical site; or
  2. North Texas is one possible site among several.
  The slide can hold either position, but it should not sound committed in the heading and provisional in the body.
- If openness is the priority, revise the heading to **“Planetary systems become consequential somewhere.”** North Texas can then appear as the present test aperture below it.
- Replace the *Groundwater Earth* webpage screenshot with a project image demonstrating Acciavatti’s sectional, temporal, or hydrological method.

**Add**

- Add one site-specific artifact: a regional utility-territory seam, floodplain crossing a proposed development, tax-incentive boundary, agricultural valuation record, or data-center interconnection request.

## Slide 7 — Counter-reading

### Current heading

**The forecast is not the only way to read a landscape.**

### Function in the argument

Introduce counter-computation as a challenge to universal legibility, certainty, separation, and institutionalized prediction.

### Speaker script

If forecasts are techniques for making futures actionable, they should be placed beside other practices of reading uncertainty. Mysticism, animism, divination, and alchemy matter here not as an escape from evidence, and not because they are interchangeable. They matter because they expose assumptions that computation often conceals: that the world can be separated into stable variables, that uncertainty is a deficit to be reduced, that relation can be made fully legible, and that prediction authorizes intervention.

The political distinction is not simply that computation is rational while divination is irrational. The more consequential distinction is institutional authority. A load forecast can reserve electricity; a property model can redirect investment; a risk score can alter access; a divinatory reading cannot ordinarily finance a road. Counter-reading therefore asks how certain predictive practices gained the authority to transform land, and what forms of relation, opacity, obligation, or unknowability might resist their claim to completeness. The aim is not to replace one oracle with another, but to make prediction answerable to what it cannot contain.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep this inquiry. It gives the project an artistic and epistemological dimension beyond familiar critiques of infrastructure.
- Keep opacity, ineffability, animism, cosmotechnics, poethics, and incomputability as a research index in the archive.
- Move the Glissant quotation from the projection slide here, where the demand for transparency is directly at issue.

**Change**

- Six theorists in six equal boxes risks flattening incompatible intellectual projects into a taxonomy of alternatives. Organize the slide around three operations instead:
  1. **refuse total legibility**;
  2. **recompose relations**;
  3. **act without mastering uncertainty**.
  Place thinkers beneath those operations rather than making each thinker a category.
- State the asymmetry between computation and divination. They may share predictive or interpretive forms, but they do not possess equivalent institutions, truth claims, or material authority.
- Replace generalized “mystical” visual language with an artwork that performs a specific counter-reading.

**Add**

- Louise Amoore’s *Cloud Ethics* can connect partiality and doubt directly to algorithmic responsibility without requiring a romance of the non-computational.
- Omar Mismar’s *I will not find this image beautiful* is appropriate if discussed as a refusal of aesthetic resolution and as a damaged relation between image, evidence, and political violence.
- Add one historical question: what did modern calculation borrow from, or define itself against, in divination, astrology, alchemy, and administrative enumeration?
- Add a methodological consequence: what would the researcher actually do differently after taking opacity or nonseparability seriously?

**Remove or avoid**

- Remove any occult imagery that merely codes the slide as mysterious. It would aestheticize the very epistemic difference being investigated.
- Avoid saying that these theorists reject reason or evidence. Their relevance is more precise: they contest singular, universal, and fully transparent accounts of reality.

## Slide 8 — Methods

### Current heading

**Read each layer beside what it omits.**

### Function in the argument

Translate the theoretical problem into a research practice combining spatial evidence, institutional records, situated observation, and performative computation.

### Speaker script

The method is comparative, but not in the sense of assembling every available layer into a more complete master map. Instead, each representation is read beside the conditions of its production and the realities it excludes. A suitability model can be reconstructed alongside the testimony it cannot encode. A utility forecast can be read beside the duration of water, soil, maintenance, and debt. A parcel history can be placed beside the uses and relations that title records do not recognize.

The work would combine spatial and quantitative records with institutional and qualitative evidence, then use computation performatively rather than only analytically. Competing models might produce incompatible sites from the same ground. An interface might disclose the weight assigned to each criterion, require an encounter with excluded evidence, or suspend calculation when the available knowledge cannot support a decision. The goal is not to create a total archive. It is to make the partiality of each account visible and consequential.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep the three methodological registers: spatial/quantitative, institutional/qualitative, and computational/performative.
- The heading is strong because it makes omission an active object of research.
- Keep uncertainty visible as a method, not merely as a visual style.

**Change**

- The lists currently read as an inventory of everything the project could collect. Specify one likely research operation under each register:
  - reconstruct a land or load forecast;
  - trace its passage through meetings and agreements;
  - build two incompatible models from the same site;
  - document the consequences that neither model represents.
- Separate **source** from **method** from **output**. “Meeting minutes” are a source; “discourse analysis” or “institutional tracing” is a method; an annotated timeline is an output.
- Explain “computational refusal” through a concrete interaction, not only a phrase.

**Add**

- Add provenance and access as methodological concerns. Which records are public, withheld, proprietary, inferred, or produced by the researcher?
- Add interview and fieldwork ethics, especially if residents, workers, or landowners become evidence within an interface.
- Nicole Starosielski’s movement from network topology to situated topography is a useful methodological precedent for following infrastructure to its landing zones.
- *Feral Atlas* is a strong precedent for moving from situated “patches” toward planetary processes without presuming a single totalizing view.

**Remove or relocate**

- The Stengers quotation should remain only if the script explains what “bridge-making” changes in the method. Otherwise the project’s own heading is more precise and should stand alone.
- Move the quipu precedent to the counter-reading slide if its main role is to pluralize computation; retain it here only if a particular representational procedure is being borrowed.

## Slide 9 — Community of practice

### Current heading

**Precedents are methods, not illustrations.**

### Function in the argument

Position the project within a community of artistic, architectural, and scholarly practices, while specifying what is learned from each.

### Speaker script

The precedent field is not intended as a mood board. Each work is valuable because it proposes a method for making infrastructure perceptible and contestable. Forensic Architecture treats images, models, testimony, and atmospheric traces as evidence that can be assembled without pretending to neutrality. *Anatomy of an AI System* follows an apparently simple object across extraction, labor, logistics, data, and waste. *Feral Atlas* links situated disturbances without resolving them into a seamless planetary overview. Nicole Starosielski follows cables to the particular beaches, histories, and maintenance practices through which a global network exists.

Other precedents offer different operations: Mimi Onuoha makes absence itself into an evidentiary object; Omar Mismar refuses the consolations of a resolved image; Ani Liu uses bodily and sensory experience to unsettle technological abstraction. The relevant question for each precedent is not “Does it address the same topic?” but “What procedure can this project test, transform, or contest?”

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep *Cloud Studies* as the principal visual precedent. It connects atmosphere, computation, evidence, installation, and public address.
- Keep the link to the larger draggable field so the slide itself does not become a complete bibliography.
- Keep Ani Liu in the broader archive; the existing local analysis gives the project a stronger basis than a single external link.

**Change**

- Replace the four-image collage with three precedents, each labeled by the method being borrowed. For example:
  - **assemble heterogeneous evidence** — Forensic Architecture;
  - **trace an object across scales** — Crawford and Joler;
  - **organize situated patches without totalization** — *Feral Atlas*.
- Use images from within the works rather than screenshots of their webpages.
- Explain why each precedent is adjacent but insufficient. Critical positioning requires difference as well as affinity.

**Add**

- Add *Feral Atlas* to the main slide or Field. It is exceptionally relevant to an open, nonlinear, multi-authored atlas that moves between local disturbances and planetary processes.
- Add Tung-Hui Hu’s *A Prehistory of the Cloud* as a theoretical precedent for linking data centers to older infrastructural and political histories.
- Add Shannon Mattern for urban media and long infrastructural duration; Nicole Starosielski for network landing zones; Louise Amoore for partiality and algorithmic doubt; and Brenna Bhandar for the legal production of property.
- In the Field, add a visible “method borrowed” field to every card. Title and author alone do not yet make it a research archive.

**Remove or demote**

- Demote *Minga* unless game mechanics or collective resource allocation become central to the covenant interface.
- Demote *A.I. Toys* unless embodied sensing or domestic computation is explicitly linked to the research operation. “Technology and art” is not a sufficient connection.

## Slide 10 — Representation

### Current heading

**The interface must show what the model suppresses.**

### Function in the argument

Define representation as an epistemic and political operation rather than a final layer of visual styling.

### Speaker script

Representation is not a neutral container for research completed elsewhere. Maps, models, atlases, and interfaces determine what can be compared, what appears simultaneous, and what is relegated to a caption or omitted altogether. If the project critiques models for suppressing uncertainty and relation, its own interface cannot claim an innocent overview.

The visual system should therefore preserve distinctions among evidence, inference, forecast, and speculation. Provenance and scale should remain visible. Conflicting accounts should be allowed to remain unresolved rather than being blended into a single synthetic surface. Interaction should also carry argumentative force: dragging might reorganize associations; changing a weight might expose who defined a criterion; opening a layer might reveal testimony or maintenance obligations; and in some cases the interface might refuse to produce a suitability result. The question is not simply how to make the research legible, but how to keep legibility from becoming another form of mastery.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep the heading. It makes the interface accountable to the project’s critique.
- Keep provenance, scale, and uncertainty as visible features.
- Keep the distinction between receiving an argument and encountering one through interaction.

**Change**

- Replace general interaction verbs—scroll, pan, drag, withhold—with one or two explicit before-and-after operations. What does the user know, confront, or become unable to do after interacting?
- Define a visual grammar for epistemic status:
  - documented evidence;
  - institutional projection;
  - researcher inference;
  - contested testimony;
  - speculative proposition;
  - unavailable or withheld information.
- Avoid treating visibility as automatically emancipatory. Some forms of opacity protect communities; some disclosures assist development or surveillance.

**Add**

- Add a small prototype frame showing one interface conflict rather than two precedent images. For example, a suitability result that fractures when testimony or water duration enters the model.
- Louise Amoore’s argument for responsibility through partiality and doubt is directly relevant here.
- Jussi Parikka’s *Operational Images* is useful if the project distinguishes images made for human interpretation from images that participate in technical operations.
- Mimi Onuoha can support a visible category of unavailable, uncollected, or institutionally illegible data.

**Remove or relocate**

- *Pink Cell Tower* should stay only if the project borrows its tactic of making hidden infrastructure physically and politically conspicuous. Otherwise it reads as topical illustration.
- The *Blacklight* quotation is evocative, but the slide must explain the representational operation of emanation rather than illumination. If that operation is not developed, move the quote to the archive.

## Slide 11 — Provisional claim

### Current heading

**Uncertainty becomes commitment.**

### Function in the argument

Condense the research territory into a working claim without presenting it as a concluded thesis.

### Speaker script

The provisional claim is that growth becomes actionable only after uncertain futures are translated into present commitments. Forecasts enter zoning categories, infrastructure plans, service agreements, financial incentives, land transactions, and design standards. Through these protocols, a future that has not arrived begins to reorganize ground.

The subdivision and the data-center campus are one possible pair through which to test this claim. The former materializes forecasts of future residents; the latter materializes forecasts of future computation. Their adjacency is analytically useful because both mobilize land, energy, water, finance, and public authority while presenting demand as an external necessity. Yet the broader claim is not that the two are equivalent, nor that all development follows one model. It is that anticipated futures are governed through protocols—and that those protocols can be reconstructed, compared, contested, and perhaps rewritten.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep “Uncertainty becomes commitment.” It is concise, precise, and broad enough to survive a change of site.
- Keep the insistence that futures are governed rather than inevitable.
- Keep the subdivision/data-center pairing as a possible test, not as the definition of the entire research field.

**Change**

- Label this explicitly as a **working proposition** rather than a rhetorical argument. That language better suits the current exploratory phase.
- Clarify that institutions do not eliminate uncertainty; they distribute and materialize it. Some parties receive predictable returns while others inherit ecological, financial, or maintenance risk.
- Ask which commitments are infrastructural, contractual, financial, ecological, or perceptual. “Commitment” gains force when its forms can be distinguished.

**Add**

- Add one counterexample or failure case. A forecast that did not materialize—but still left roads, debt, land speculation, or stranded capacity—would make the claim testable.
- Add the concept of path dependency carefully: early commitments constrain later choices without making them absolutely inevitable.
- If the subdivision/data-center comparison remains, show one shared protocol and one decisive difference. This will prevent an overly elegant analogy.

**Remove or avoid**

- Avoid language implying that every institution acts as a unified author of growth. The research should track disagreement among utilities, municipalities, developers, residents, regulators, and ecological systems.

## Slide 12 — Potential capstone

### Current heading

**An atlas that keeps the decision open.**

### Function in the argument

Translate the research into a possible design form while preserving room for the thesis to change.

### Speaker script

One possible capstone is an interactive and performable atlas that follows a forecast across scales. At a regional scale, it could trace the networks and institutional territories that make a site possible. At a municipal or corridor scale, it could show how planning, utility, economic-development, and ecological records converge without becoming commensurable. At a parcel scale, it could reconstruct the sequence through which ground became property, property became suitable, capacity became reservable, and forecast became commitment.

The atlas would not produce a single correct answer about where development should occur. Its central experiment would be to keep incompatible definitions of suitability in view. A covenant-like interface might introduce obligations before calculation: testimony, ecological duration, water limits, maintenance, or uncertainty could alter the user’s ability to proceed. In some cases the system might withhold a result. The capstone is therefore less a decision tool than an investigation into the conditions under which a decision can claim legitimacy.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep the atlas as one candidate form. It accommodates archival, spatial, computational, and performative research.
- Keep multiple scales, provided they all follow the same object or process.
- Keep the ambition to prevent the atlas from issuing a final verdict.

**Change**

- Present the atlas as a hypothesis, not the inevitable capstone. At this phase, the form should remain accountable to what the research discovers.
- The three scales currently return strongly to North Texas. If the research remains broad, rewrite them as `network / jurisdiction / ground` or `system / protocol / site`.
- Define “covenant” carefully. The word carries theological, legal, property, and settler-colonial histories; it cannot function only as an appealing synonym for friction.

**Add**

- Add three small prototype questions rather than one resolved interface:
  1. Can two incompatible suitability models coexist without synthesis?
  2. Can testimony or ecological duration change what the model is permitted to calculate?
  3. Can the interface show the commitments already embedded in an apparently open choice?
- Add an account of the audience. Is the atlas for a public presentation, a planning dispute, a gallery installation, an academic reader, or several publics encountering different versions?
- Add a maintenance question: who updates the atlas, and what happens when its evidence becomes obsolete?

**Remove or defer**

- Defer the claim that calculation will be prevented until a convincing rule, authority, and consequence can be demonstrated. Otherwise refusal risks becoming theatrical.
- Avoid promising regional, municipal, and parcel completeness simultaneously. One carefully reconstructed chain is more credible than an encyclopedic platform.

## Slide 13 — Negotiation

### Current heading

**Can a map become a site of negotiation before a forecast becomes a commitment?**

### Function in the argument

End with an open but consequential design question that returns representation to governance.

### Speaker script

The project closes with a question rather than a prescription. Maps have long been instruments through which property, capacity, risk, and development are made administratively real. Calling a map a site of negotiation does not make it one. Negotiation requires parties with standing, access to evidence, the ability to contest categories, and some capacity to alter the outcome.

The question is therefore not whether an interactive map can appear participatory. It is whether representation can intervene before uncertain futures harden into commitments—and whether that intervention redistributes authority rather than only visualizing disagreement. This remains the project’s open territory: to understand how forecasts build, how their assumptions become material, and under what conditions a different account of the landscape might still matter.

### Editorial assessment

**Keep**

- Keep a closing question rather than a summary slide.
- Keep the link outward to the Field and Index. They demonstrate that the presentation is an entrance into an active archive.

**Change**

- Make the question slightly more incisive: **“Under what conditions can a map become a site of negotiation before a forecast becomes a commitment?”** “Under what conditions” prevents the deck from assuming that design alone produces negotiation.
- Consider allowing a brief pause with no additional imagery. The question should remain visually dominant.

**Add**

- In speech, name the minimum conditions of negotiation: standing, evidence, time, contestability, and capacity to alter an outcome.
- If a final image is desired, return to the fragment from the opening but now reveal the conflicting boundaries or accounts it contains. This would give the deck a visual, not merely verbal, closure.

**Remove or avoid**

- Avoid ending with the interface as the hero. The research concerns institutional authority and material commitments; the interface is one possible instrument within that larger field.

# Questions to carry into the next semester

- Is the primary object a forecast, a parcel, a protocol, or an atlas? Each can organize the thesis, but not all can remain equally central.
- What is the minimum situated case required to make a planetary claim responsibly?
- Which forecast has sufficient documentary evidence to be reconstructed from projection through commitment?
- What form of uncertainty is at stake: statistical uncertainty, institutional disagreement, ecological indeterminacy, withheld information, or irreducible unknowability?
- Who has the authority to declare a site suitable, and who has standing to make it unsuitable?
- What would it mean for an interface to redistribute authority rather than only represent conflict?
- When is opacity protective, and when does it shield institutional power?
- Which commitment is the project trying to intervene before—and what would make that intervention materially consequential?
